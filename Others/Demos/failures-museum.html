01 · The premise

Do not hide the broken thing.

The most dangerous thing about a mistake is often not the mistake itself. It is what happens afterward: the story becomes simplified, the evidence disappears, the unusual warning becomes ordinary, and everyone remembers the final error while forgetting the chain that made the error possible.

This museum reverses that process.

Every exhibit begins at the end: the collapsed structure, the lost spacecraft, the failed experiment, the confusing interface, the unsafe machine, the wrong calculation, or the decision that looked reasonable until the consequences arrived.

Visitors then work backward. What was expected? What actually happened? Which assumption changed? Which barrier should have caught the problem? Why did it not? What would have made the mistake visible sooner?

How to use the museum

Read the failed result before opening the reconstruction. Make your own hypothesis. Then reveal the mechanism.

The objective is not to identify someone to blame. The objective is to understand causality well enough that the same class of failure becomes harder to repeat.

03 · Historical corridor

Mistakes across time

The history of progress is also a history of incorrect models, failed experiments, broken mechanisms, mistaken assumptions, and increasingly sophisticated ways of detecting them.

~300 BCE · ANCIENT WORLD

Measurement begins defeating intuition

Ancient mathematical and physical investigations demonstrated a recurring pattern: quantities that feel obvious can behave differently when measured. The transition from intuition to measurement is one of humanity's oldest error-correction tools.

1600s · SCIENTIFIC REVOLUTION

Observation becomes an argument

Controlled experiments, quantitative measurement, and repeatability increasingly provided methods for distinguishing competing explanations.

1840s · MEDICINE

Handwashing and the invisible cause

Observations associated with Ignaz Semmelweis demonstrated that changing a procedure could dramatically change outcomes, even before the underlying mechanism was understood in modern terms.

1940 · STRUCTURAL ENGINEERING

Tacoma Narrows

A spectacular bridge collapse became an enduring lesson in dynamic loading, aerodynamic behavior, structural modeling, and the difference between static strength and stability.

1960s–1980s · SYSTEMS ERA

Safety becomes a systems discipline

Large technical systems made it increasingly clear that accidents could emerge from interactions among hardware, software, people, procedures, organizations, workload, and environment.

1990s–TODAY · SOFTWARE

Code becomes physical infrastructure

Software now controls vehicles, factories, spacecraft, medicine, finance, communications, and everyday devices. A semantic software error can therefore become a physical-world failure.

05 · Failure analysis

Anatomy of a mistake

A useful investigation separates what happened from why it happened. It also avoids assuming that every complex failure has one single “root cause.”

01

Result

Start with observable facts. What actually happened? What changed from the intended state?

02

Conditions

Identify environment, timing, workload, inputs, equipment condition, software state, and organizational context.

03

Mechanism

Find the physical, logical, biological, or social mechanism connecting conditions to the failed result.

04

Barrier

Ask what should have prevented, detected, contained, or mitigated the failure.

05

Assumption

Locate what someone or something was implicitly assuming: range, unit, behavior, timing, reliability, or human response.

06

Feedback

Determine whether the system provided enough information for people to recognize that reality was diverging from expectation.

07

Counterfactual

Ask which realistic change would have interrupted the chain before the harmful outcome.

08

Lesson

Convert the analysis into a design principle, test, checklist item, interface improvement, or new measurement.

06 · Deep archive

Case-study room

These are deliberately simplified educational reconstructions. Real incident investigations contain substantially more evidence, disagreement, technical detail, and historical context.

Engineering

Tacoma Narrows: strength was not the whole problem

A common simplified story says the bridge “wasn't strong enough.” That misses the central educational point. Structures do not merely experience loads; they respond to them.

Wind can interact with movement. Movement changes airflow. Changed airflow changes forces. Those forces can increase movement.

The museum lesson is therefore: whenever a system can move, vibrate, oscillate, rotate, resonate, or otherwise respond dynamically, static calculations may answer only part of the safety question.

Software

Mars Climate Orbiter: numbers have meanings

Software can exchange a perfectly valid numeric value while disagreeing completely about what that value represents.

Ten meters and ten feet are both legitimate numbers. Ten newton-seconds and ten pound-force-seconds are both legitimate numbers. Their physical meanings are not interchangeable.

A strong software interface therefore defines semantics, not merely syntax.

Design

New Coke: experiments have boundaries

A blind taste test can be a good experiment while still being inadequate for a product strategy.

The experimental question might be: “Which formulation is preferred when brand identity is hidden?”

The business question might instead be: “What happens when millions of people lose the familiar product they associate with a brand?”

Those are different experiments.

Human factors

The door: predictable user error is evidence

When a person pushes a pull door, the simplest explanation is often “the person made a mistake.”

But if thousands of people make the same mistake, the repetition is itself evidence about the design.

A good interface does not require perfect users. It communicates intended action through form, position, resistance, labeling, feedback, and consequence.

07 · Museum examination

Can you diagnose the mistake?

01. A software module expects meters per second. Another supplies kilometers per hour. The values look plausible. What is the primary problem?

Units are part of the meaning of data. The interface contract is ambiguous or violated.✓ A syntactically valid number can still be semantically wrong.

02. A product wins a blind taste test but performs badly after a major rebrand. What did the test most likely exclude?

Context, identity, expectation, habit, and brand meaning.✓ The test deliberately isolated taste. The real-world experience contained variables the experiment removed.

03. A recurring safety anomaly has happened many times without producing a catastrophe. What conclusion is justified?

The system survived those exposures; that does not prove the mechanism is safe.✓ Previous survival provides evidence about past outcomes, not proof that a hazardous mechanism has become harmless.

04. A person repeatedly pushes a door that should be pulled. What should a designer investigate first?

What visual or physical cue communicates the intended action.✓ Repeated predictable user errors can reveal an interface that communicates the wrong action.

05. A component works perfectly in its original system and fails after being reused in another. What should be investigated?

Whether assumptions about inputs, range, timing, environment, and behavior changed.✓ Reusing code transfers assumptions along with implementation. A component is only reusable when its assumptions remain valid.
08 · Field guide

Failure-analysis glossary

A small vocabulary for thinking clearly about complex failures.

Assumption

A belief treated as true for a design, model, plan, or decision.

Barrier

A mechanism intended to prevent, detect, contain, or mitigate an unwanted event.

Drift

Gradual movement away from intended operating conditions.

Failure mode

A specific way a component or process can fail to perform its function.

Feedback

Information about actual performance that can alter future behavior.

Human factors

The study of interactions among people, technology, tasks, environments, and organizations.

Latent condition

A hidden system condition that can exist before an incident and increase failure likelihood or severity.

Near miss

An event that could have caused harm but did not.

Normalization

Gradual acceptance of abnormal conditions because previous exposures did not produce visible consequences.

Redundancy

Additional independent capability intended to preserve function when another element fails.

Safety margin

The distance between normal operation and an unacceptable limit.

Root cause

A phrase often used for deeper causal conditions, though complex failures may have multiple interacting causes.

Interface

A boundary where components, people, teams, or systems exchange information, energy, or responsibility.

Coupling

A relationship where behavior in one part of a system affects behavior in another.

Validation

Evidence that a system meets the intended real-world need or behavior.

Verification

Evidence that an implementation satisfies specified requirements.

09 · The museum method

Seven questions for any mistake.

1. What happened?

Describe observations before assigning explanations. Separate the event from the story about the event.

2. What was expected?

Define the intended behavior. A failure can only be identified relative to some expectation.

3. What changed?

Identify differences in input, environment, timing, equipment, software, workload, procedure, or organizational context.

4. What mechanism connects them?

Build the causal chain. Do not stop at “X happened before Y.” Ask how X physically or logically produced Y.

5. What should have detected it?

Find alarms, tests, inspections, reviews, measurements, human observations, or missing signals.

6. Why did the barriers fail?

Investigate assumptions, interfaces, workload, incentives, training, environment, maintenance, design, and communication.

7. What would make recurrence harder?

Prefer changes that improve the system itself instead of depending exclusively on people remembering to behave perfectly.

The museum rule

Never end an investigation with “someone should have been more careful.” Ask what made the unsafe action understandable, possible, invisible, or difficult to recover from.
